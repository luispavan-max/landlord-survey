Survey Analysis & Strategic Findings

Landlord Card Acceptance Hinges on Embedded, Low-Fee Solutions—Not on Overcoming Rejection

Six headline findings

  1. Acceptance is ahead of behavior. 58% of landlords accept cards, but debit + credit combined represent only 17% of actual rent collected. Among acceptors, half say fewer than 50% of tenants actually use cards. The challenge is not enabling acceptance — it's driving usage.
  2. Canada is structurally different. Only 44% of Canadian landlords accept both card types, vs. 64% in the US and 63% in UAE. Interac e-Transfer carries 35% of Canadian rent, filling the digital role that cards play elsewhere. A card strategy for Canada must displace a working digital rail, not just paper.
  3. Cost is the decisive constraint, not complexity. 62% won't absorb more than $10 on $1,000 rent. Among non-acceptors, 41% demand the tenant pays the full fee. The data strongly supports tenant-paid or low-landlord-cost structures, particularly for converting non-acceptors.
  4. UAE is the highest-pain, highest-readiness market. UAE landlords report moderate-or-worse collection problems at rates 9–30pp above US peers, yet 86% say they'd activate card acceptance if offered at no cost. UAE appears particularly receptive.
  5. Ancillary charges are the 90%+ wedge. For deposits, application fees, first/last month, and damage costs, 89–95% of landlords either already accept cards, say they would be willing, or might consider doing so depending on the fee. These one-time charges provide a useful entry point before expanding to recurring rent.
  6. 43% of landlords not currently accepting cards have unactivated capability or are unsure whether it is available. 22% say card acceptance is available but hadn't thought about it; another 22% are unsure. This points to a meaningful awareness and activation opportunity.
Methodology

Survey design and sample

240 landlords were surveyed via Pollfish's consumer panel on September 15–16, 2026, across three markets: United States (n=100), Canada (n=70), and United Arab Emirates (n=70). The survey included 44 questions covering landlord profile, property characteristics, current payment methods, card acceptance status, barriers, fee sensitivity, feature preferences, pain points, trust preferences, and experience with specialty rent platforms.

Screening questions ensured all respondents own or manage residential rental property and have involvement in deciding how tenants pay rent. Respondents who selected "No input" on payment decisions or "None of the above" on property ownership were screened out.

Cross-tabulation approach: This analysis goes beyond the Pollfish AI-generated report by crossing key survey variables — card acceptance status, country, portfolio size, and management type — against behavioral, attitudinal, and pain-point questions. Heatmap tables throughout use color intensity to indicate relative magnitude within each table, with green tones for opportunity, red for pain/barriers, and blue for contextual metrics.

Limitations: The sample of 240 is directionally informative but not statistically representative of each national landlord population. Sub-group cross-tabs (especially non-acceptors, n=51, and never-accepted, n=43) should be treated as indicative patterns rather than precise estimates. Country-specific methods (Interac, Zelle, CareemPay) were shown only to respondents in the relevant market.

Chapter 10

Strategic Recommendations

Six priorities for driving card-based rent acceptance

The data points to a market that is ready in principle but held back by cost concerns, operational inertia, and awareness gaps. The following recommendations are grounded in the cross-tabulation evidence above, prioritized by addressability and impact.

1. Minimize landlord-borne card costs

62% will absorb no more than $10 on a $1,000 rent payment, and 41% of non-acceptors require zero landlord cost. At a hypothetical 2% landlord-borne fee, 59% of current acceptors and 71% of non-acceptors report fee ceilings below that level; at 3%, the shares rise to 78% and 88%. This strongly supports tenant-paid, shared-fee, or otherwise low-landlord-cost structures.

All marketsProduct design

2. Embed card acceptance into banks and PM platforms — don't build standalone

29% trust their bank most for rent collection; only 4% trust a dedicated rent app. 81% would activate if offered through their existing bank/software at no cost. The distribution strategy should be embedded: integrate with bank apps, PM software (Yardi, AppFolio, Buildium in the US; RentCafe/MRI in UAE), and accounting tools (QuickBooks in the US/Canada). The standalone app play is structurally disadvantaged.

All marketsDistribution

3. Start with ancillary charges as the wedge, then expand to recurring rent

91–95% of landlords are at least open to card acceptance for deposits, application fees, first/last month rent, and damage costs, including respondents who would consider it depending on the fee. These one-time charges provide a useful entry point for testing card acceptance before expanding to recurring rent. As landlords build comfort with card payments in this context, the pathway to recurring monthly rent becomes shorter and more credible.

All marketsGo-to-market

4. Test activation prompts for landlords with dormant card capability

43% of landlords not currently accepting cards either report that card acceptance is already available but they hadn’t thought about activating it (22%), or are unsure whether their current provider offers it (22%). In the US, 33% say the capability is available but had not been considered. This creates a clear opportunity to test activation prompts within existing banking and property-management workflows.

US priorityUAE priorityActivation

5. Lead messaging with operational benefits, not payment choice

The four decision-driving features are all practical: cost protection (38%), embedded access (29%), collection toolkit (28%), and auto bookkeeping (25%). "Tenant credit building" — despite 74% broad interest — is chosen as a priority by only 9%. UAE landlords most value "helps me get paid on time" (60%). Frame card acceptance as reducing late payments, automating records, and speeding settlement — not as adding a payment option.

All marketsMessaging

6. Localize the go-to-market by country

The three markets have fundamentally different payment landscapes, trust hierarchies, and barrier profiles:

🇺🇸 US: Most fragmented payment mix. Dual-channel distribution (banks + PM companies). Highest chargeback concern among non-acceptors (42%). Strongest activation opportunity (33% have features available but unused). Lead with chargeback protection + embedded PM integration.

🇨🇦 Canada: Interac e-Transfer carries 35% of rent — any card proposition must explain what it offers beyond this working digital rail. Highest self-management rate (69%). Trust splits between banks (26%) and accountants (17%). Canada also has the most non-acceptors (33%) and the most who prefer to keep collection in their own hands (14%). QuickBooks and accounting-software integration may matter more than PM platforms here.

🇦🇪 UAE: Highest pain on every metric (bounced payments at 64%, chasing at 57%). Highest activation willingness (86%). Bank trust dominates (39%). "Helps me get paid on time" is the #1 adoption reason (60%). Despite this, 75% of non-acceptors say "current methods work fine" — the strongest inertia signal. This is the market showing the highest stated activation likelihood if banks lead the offering, paired with messaging about settlement speed and payment certainty.

Suggested next steps

Immediate (0–3 months): Quantify the activation opportunity — how many landlords on existing platforms have dormant card features, and what would a prompt-to-activate test look like in each market?

Near-term (3–6 months): Design and pilot an ancillary-charge card acceptance program (deposits + application fees) through 1–2 bank or PM platform partners per market, with tenant-pays fee model.

Medium-term (6–12 months): Expand to recurring rent for landlords who adopted via ancillary charges, leveraging the collection-toolkit and auto-bookkeeping features they now rely on.

Ongoing research: Run a parallel tenant-side survey to quantify willingness to pay card fees, preferred payment methods, and the impact of credit-building features on payment choice. The landlord survey reveals demand — the tenant survey quantifies how much they'd pay for it.
